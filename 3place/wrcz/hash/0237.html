语言是什么？

文/韩宝龙

2026年11月10日 06时07分56秒

最近在读英文版的《悲惨世界》，在给初一的儿子讲解数学题，在和儿子一起读他们的语文书中的诗词。

然后，我在想，语言究竟是什么？

我在给儿子讲数学时，曾给他说过：“数学也不过是一门语言，而且是一种‘人为’的语言，也就是人创造、发明出来的语言。而数学对应的，并不是物理，而是语文，因为语文背后的‘汉语’，则是‘自然发展出来的语言’。”

这个说法非常‘哲学’，因为我在试图定义‘语言’。

那么，什么是语言呢？

这就要从语言的功能说起，毕竟，语言是一个‘功能性工具’。

一个最直接、最简洁的定义就是：语言就是用于交流的工具。

这个定义，有两层意思：

  1. 只要是语言，就可以用来交流。

  2. 只要可以交流，就是语言。

比如，汉语，是用于‘中国人，或者汉文化人之间交流的’工具。

数学，是用于‘人和人之间交流对自然的理解的’工具。

英语，是用于‘英语国家人之间，以及英语国家外人对文化、信息需求的’工具。

再比如软件领域的“Python”语言，是用于‘人（程序员）和电脑里的编译器之间交流’的工具。

这些（汉语、数学、英语、Python等）都是语言，又都不是，因为白马非马。

它们都是语言的一部分，而不是语言本身。

那么，什么是语言呢？

一个更简洁，更直接的定义就来了：一切用于交流的工具的整体，被称为语言。

是的，汉语、英语、数学，都是语言的一部分，它们的整体就是语言。

当你开始琢磨时，你会发现，汉语和英语是有交集的，比如“坦克”这个来自英语 tank 的词，现在也属于汉语的一部分了。再比如‘台风’，其实是英语‘typhoon’，德语‘Taifun’，法语‘typhon’，拉丁语‘typhon’的同一个表达，这个就是这些语言的‘交集’。

最直接的、容易想到的交集，肯定是‘爸爸、妈妈’了。

汉语中的‘平行、绝对值’等，则是汉语和数学的交集了。或者说，是数学从汉语中‘借’过来的，而这个‘借’，通常是‘扩大原字、词的意思’，或者创造一个新的字词，比如砼，这个就是创造了一个字，用于表达建筑行业。

对了，‘建筑行业用语’，也是‘一门语言’。

现在，我们对语言有了比较直接的定义了：用于交流的工具。这个交流的主语通常是‘人’，但不一定。

比如，人和狗之间，人和机器之间，机器和机器之间，都有语言。

那么，如何细分语言呢？这就需要一层一层细分了。

第一层就是：自然语言和人为语言。

自然语言，就是自然发展起来的，世界上各地人日常说的话，都是自然语言。

自然语言非常丰富，毕竟人类已经出现了几百万年，有记录的文明也有几千年了。

每一个主要文明的自然语言，基本上都包括了衣食住行，以及主要的文明形式，比如音乐、美术、情感、建筑、动物、数学以及一部分医学、科学等。

人为语言，则是人类为了某个特定领域的交流，为了避免歧义，提高交流效率而在自然语言习惯基础上发展出来的一门语言。

比如‘马’，作为古代重要的工具，人和人之间为了交流‘马’的信息，在‘文字’中发展出了一系列的字。

比如马本身：优劣、年龄、性别：驹、骒、骘、骥、骏、驽、骀、骠、骁、骜；

马的毛色：骊、骓、骝、骅、骐、骢、骠、骃、骍、骆；

马的动作与状态：驰、骋、骤、腾、驱、驶、驻、驯、骇、惊、骚、骄、骗、驳、蓦、骛、骧；

马的驾车、马具、制度等：驾、驭、驷、骖、驸、驿、驺、驵、驮、骟；

相关的牲畜：骡、驴、骆、骉；

其他和马有关的：鞍、鞭、缰、辔、勒、策、镫、厩、秣、刍、骄、骇、惊、骗、驳、驿、驸。

这么多字，虽然也属于汉字，但和普通人没有什么关系，只有‘从业人员’才需要。

如果普通人去研究、记忆这些，非常没有必要，也很困难，这就像孔乙己说的‘回字有四种写法’了。

所以，虽然这些字‘属于’汉语，其实并不完全，它应该是属于‘马语’：用于交流‘和马相关信息’的。

所以，如果你是喜欢中国文化，却不认识上面那些字，不要内疚，因为，它们只是‘寄生在汉语’中的‘马语’而已。

就像寄生在英语中的‘Python’语言一样：英语国家的人，不会为不懂Python语言而内疚吧！

你看，我们现在勾勒出了‘语言’这个整体面貌：

  1. 它就像一片沼泽地，离得远了看，它们是连成一片的；

  2. 走近后看，它们是一个一个的自然语言组成的区域，只是这些自然语言之间直接或者因为交流有着千丝万缕的连接。

  3. 再走近，你就发现每一个自然语言的区域中，又有很多的起伏不平，每一个小的区域又有各自的精彩。

  4. 抬头看，你又会发现，空中还有着‘海市蜃楼’，据说，这是不同区域的精彩在‘光的折射下’，形成的美好，比如Python这一类。就是汇集了各种文化的特点而形成的。

对于我们普通人来说，我们日常用的，不过是自然语言极少的一部分，以及某一种或者几种某些专业领域的语言而已。

比如，我日常用的是部分汉语、部分英语、部分计算机语言（其中包括Shell、Python等）以及一些管理领域的语言。

那么，我们是基于什么原则来选用语言呢？

这取决于我们自己，以及语言了。

首先，我们都有自己的‘母语’，母语基本上覆盖了我们的婴幼儿、童年以及基础教育。

所以，我们会‘更擅长’用母语来表达自己，是的，是‘表达自己’。

其次，我们在成长的过程中，总是会‘遇到’，对，是‘遇到’一些我们感兴趣的领域，我们会熟练掌握这个领域的‘语言’，比如‘动物领域’，‘围棋领域’，‘数学领域’，’打牌领域’等等。

每次进入一个新的‘语言领域’，对我们自己来说，都是一个‘全新的’开始。

在这个领域，我们需要了解新的规则。

好在，人为的语言，特别是‘特定领域人为的语言’，通常都比较简单，而且会‘继承’派生它的自然语言的主要特征。所以，你可以在很短的时间里了解一门‘人为语言’，这个‘很短的时间’，可以是几天、几个小时、甚至几分钟。

当然，你要完全‘掌握’这门语言，还是需要更多的时间的，毕竟，语言的目的是‘交流’，为了交流，你需要的并不是去‘了解’这个语言，而是‘用这个语言和别人交流的规则’。

比如，我们为什么要学习数学？因为我们需要‘数学’这个语言去‘理解、表达’现实世界，以及和‘其他人交流我们对世界的看法’。

那么，不学数学行不行？

不行，因为，如果不学数学这门语言，我们现有的语言（母语中的生活用语）是不足以让我们理解这个世界，并且和其他人有效交流的。

这就是我们‘学习语言’的主要动力和唯一目的。

当然，也有人‘学习语言’是为了‘装逼’，比如孔乙己学习‘回字的四种写法’，这其实就是装逼。

再比如，我刚才秀的马的那么多‘字’。（那些字，其实已经是一个语言了）。

最后，让我们落眼看一下‘英语’，我们从小学到大的‘英语’，我们学了这么多年，还是‘学不好’的英语。

现在，让我们理解一下，’英语’是什么？

我简单整理一下：

  1. 英语是生活在英国这个地方的人群中发展出来的自然语言。

  2. 随着英国的崛起和殖民，英语被带到了美洲、亚洲、非洲、澳洲等很多国家，并在许多国家代替了原来的自然语言。

  3. 在发展过程中，英语不断从拉丁语、法语、德语中‘借’一些字、词过来，这个‘借’的原因有很多，其中之一就是和这些地区文化的融合。

  4. 在文艺复兴阶段，在迎接机械、医学、美学、建筑学等科学领域的发展时，英语极速膨胀，发展出了很多新的语言，或者说语言得到了极大的扩充。

所以，当我们在‘学习英语’时，其实是有两种完全不同的行为目的的：

  1. 学习英语的日常交流能力。这是真正的‘学习英语’，是为了和英语国家的人交流。

  2. 为了学习某个以英语为载体的领域，比如‘管理学’、‘软件工程’、’操作系统’等。

当然，这两部分，很难完全分开。

再比如，我读《悲惨世界》，每天都会遇见‘生词’，这个‘生词’，大部分都是‘某个领域的专业术语’而已，我为了读懂《悲惨世界》，就需要了解这些词。

那么，我为什么要读懂《悲惨世界》呢？大概是为了了解雨果这个人，或者说1815年的法国人，他们经历了什么，而这些经历，对于我来说有什么借鉴。

那么，我读中文版的可以么？我本来以为可以，现在我知道不行。

因为，我在这次同时读中英文版的《悲惨世界》（中文版是李丹翻译，英文版是Christine翻译）时，我发现这完全是两本书，可以简单推测，法文版的又是第三本书了。

所以，现在知道了，如果我要真正了解1815年法国人的经历，他们的思想的经历，我就必须懂英语，或者法语，必须懂他们的‘心理学’方面的那些术语，必须懂‘马车相关’的英语。

必须懂那时的人需要面对的‘法律语言’，不然我就没法理解‘yellow passport’为什么给‘Jean Valjean’带来那么大的影响。

而这些，我只了解‘自然语言’是无效的。

我必须了解这个自然语言中一些更细分的领域（法律、社会、交通等）。

以上，就是我对语言的理解。而这个理解，也是我现在使用、学习语言的理论基础。

ENGLISH VERSION（GROK)

Rewrite:

I’ve been reading the English edition of Les Misérables, explaining math problems to my junior high son, and reading poetry with him from his Chinese language textbook.

While doing these things, I’ve been thinking deeply about what language actually is.

When I was explaining math to my son, I once told him something like this: “Math isn’t just a subject — it’s a language, and a very special kind of language. It’s a human-made language that we invented and designed. But it isn’t about physics. It’s about Chinese — because Chinese, the natural language behind our everyday language, is the one that developed organically.”

This idea is quite philosophical because I’m trying to define language itself.

So, what exactly is language?

We should start from its function. After all, language is a functional tool.

The simplest and most direct definition is: Language is the tool for communication.

This definition has two layers:

  1. Anything that can be used for communication is language.
  2. Anything that can be used for communication is language.

For example:
- Chinese is the tool for people within Chinese culture (or Han culture) to communicate.
- Math is the tool for people to understand and express their thoughts about the natural world.
- English is the tool for people in English-speaking countries to communicate with each other, and also for people outside those countries who need to understand their culture and information.

Another example: Python in the software world is the tool for programmers to communicate with compilers inside computers.

All of them — Chinese, math, English, Python, etc. — are languages, yet none of them are “language” itself. They are parts of language, not language in its entirety. Just like the saying “white horse is not a horse,” they are different.

So, what is language?

A simpler and more direct definition is: Language is the whole set of tools used for communication.

Yes, Chinese, English, math — they are all parts of language, and their collective whole is language.

When you start to think about it, you’ll realize that Chinese and English have intersections. For example, the word “tank” comes from English tank and is now part of Chinese. Another example is “typhoon,” which comes from English typhoon, German Taifun, French typhon, and Latin typhon — they are the same word, just in different languages.

The most obvious and immediate intersection is probably “father” and “mother.”

Words like “parallel” and “absolute value” in Chinese are intersections between Chinese and math — math has borrowed from Chinese and often expanded or invented new terms. For instance, “砼” (concrete) is a word created specifically for the construction industry.

By the way, professional language used in the construction industry is also a language.

So, we now have a pretty direct definition of language: a tool for communication. The subject of this communication is usually “people,” but not necessarily.

For example, there is language between humans and dogs, between humans and machines, and even between machines and machines.

So, how do we break language down further? We need to subdivide it layer by layer.

The first layer is natural language and artificial language.

Natural language is the everyday speech that developed organically in different regions. It is very rich because humans have existed for hundreds of thousands of years, and recorded civilizations have lasted for thousands of years. Every major civilization’s natural language covers daily life (food, clothing, housing, transportation), as well as major cultural forms such as music, art, emotion, architecture, animals, mathematics, and even parts of medicine and science.

Artificial language, on the other hand, is developed from natural language habits to serve a specific field’s communication needs — to avoid ambiguity and improve efficiency.

Take “horse” as an example. As an ancient important tool, people needed to communicate information about horses in writing. This led to a whole series of specialized terms:

  • Age, sex, quality: 驹 (foal), 骒 (young horse), 骘, 骥, 骏, 驽 (poor horse), 骀, 骠, 骁, 骜
  • Coat color: 骊, 骓, 骝, 骅, 骐, 骢, 骠, 骃, 骍, 骆
  • Actions and states: 驰, 骋, 骤, 腾, 驱, 驶, 驻, 驯, 骇, 惊, 骚, 骄, 骗, 驳, 蓦, 骛, 骧
  • Driving and equipment: 驾, 驭, 驷, 骖, 驸, 驿, 驺, 驵, 驮, 骟
  • Related animals: 骡, 驴, 骆, 骉
  • Other terms: 鞍, 鞭, 缰, 辔, 勒, 策, 镫, 厩, 秣, 刍, 骄, 骇, 惊, 骗, 驳, 驿, 驸

These are all Chinese characters, but they have almost nothing to do with ordinary people — only those in the horse-related industry would need them. If an ordinary person were to study and memorize them, it would be pointless and very difficult, just like Kong Yiji’s famous line about the four different ways to write the character “回.”

So, although these words belong to Chinese, they don’t fully belong to it. They are actually part of “horse language” — a specialized language for communicating information about horses.

So, if you like Chinese culture but don’t know any of these words, don’t feel guilty. They are not “parasitic” in Chinese — they are part of “马语” (horse language).

It’s the same as how Python is part of English — English-speaking people don’t feel guilty for not knowing Python.

So, this is how we can picture the overall picture of language:

  1. From a distance, it looks like a big swamp — one connected whole.
  2. When you get closer, you see they are separate natural language regions, but these regions are connected in countless ways through direct contact or through communication.
  3. When you get even closer, you realize that within each natural language region there are many undulating parts, and each small region has its own beauty.
  4. Looking up, you can see “mirages” in the air — these are the intersections of different regions’ beauties, like Python, which gathers characteristics from many cultures and forms something new.

For ordinary people, we only use a very small part of natural language plus a few specialized professional languages.

For example, I use part of Chinese, part of English, part of computer languages (including Shell and Python), and some management-related languages.

So, on what principle do we choose which language to use?

It depends on us and on the language itself.

First, we all have our mother tongue, which basically covers our early childhood and basic education. So we tend to be more skilled at expressing ourselves in our mother tongue.

Second, as we grow, we always encounter areas we’re interested in. We gradually master the language of that field — for example, animal language, Go language, math language, card-playing language, etc.

Every time we enter a new “language field,” it feels like a completely fresh beginning for us.

In that field, we need to learn new rules.

Fortunately, artificial languages — especially domain-specific artificial languages — are usually relatively simple and inherit the main features of the natural language they are based on. So you can learn an artificial language in a very short time — it can be days, hours, or even minutes.

Of course, fully mastering a language still takes more time. After all, the purpose of language is communication. To communicate, you don’t need to “understand” the language — you need to master the rules for using it to communicate with others.

For example, why do we learn math? Because we need the math language to understand and express the real world, and to communicate our thoughts with others.

So, is it necessary to learn math?

Yes, because if we don’t learn this language, our existing language (everyday spoken Chinese) is not enough for us to understand the world or effectively communicate with others.

This is the main driving force and only purpose of learning language.

Of course, some people learn language just to show off. Like Kong Yiji studying the four ways to write “回,” that was just showing off.

Another example is the many “words” I just showed you about horses. (Those words actually form their own language.)

Finally, let’s look at English. We’ve been studying English from primary school through high school for many years, yet we still can’t really master English.

Now, let’s understand what English really is. I’ll break it down simply:

  1. English is the natural language that developed among people who lived in England.
  2. With Britain’s rise and colonial expansion, English was carried to the Americas, Asia, Africa, Australia, and many other countries, where it replaced local languages.
  3. During its development, English constantly borrowed words and expressions from Latin, French, German, etc. One important reason was cultural fusion with those regions.
  4. During the Renaissance, as science fields like mechanics, medicine, aesthetics, and architecture expanded, English exploded and developed many new words or greatly expanded its vocabulary.

So, when we “learn English,” there are actually two completely different purposes:

  1. Learning everyday communication skills in English — this is genuine “learning English,” for communicating with English-speaking people.
  2. Learning a field that uses English as its carrier, such as management, software engineering, operating systems, etc.

Of course, these two are hard to separate completely.

For example, when I read Les Misérables, every day I encounter new words. Most of these words are professional terms from specific fields. To understand Les Misérables, I need to understand these terms.

So, why do I want to understand Les Misérables? Probably to learn about Hugo and the people of 1815 — what they experienced, and what lessons this has for me.

But can I just read the Chinese version? I used to think so. Now I know I can’t.

Because I’m currently reading the English edition (translated by Christine) and the Chinese edition (translated by Li Dan) at the same time, and they feel like two completely different books. It’s easy to guess that the French original is a third book.

So, if I truly want to understand the experiences of people in 1815 France — their thoughts and ideas — I need to know English or French, and I need to understand their psychological terminology, their carriage-related language, and their legal language. Otherwise, I won’t be able to understand why the “yellow passport” had such a huge impact on Jean Valjean.

And if I only understand the natural language, that’s not enough.

I must understand the more specialized fields within that natural language (law, society, transportation, etc.).

This is my understanding of language. And this understanding is also the theoretical foundation for how I currently use and learn language.

Reflections on Language: A Personal Inquiry

Lately, I’ve been reading the English edition of Les Misérables, helping my seventh-grade son with his math homework, and reading classical poetry with him from his school Chinese textbook.

Naturally, this led me to wonder: What, fundamentally, is language?

While explaining math to my son, I told him: “Mathematics is nothing more than a language—an artificial language created and invented by humans. And its counterpart isn’t physics; it’s literature. Because the Chinese language that underpins our literature is a natural language that evolved organically.”

It sounds quite philosophical, but that’s because I was attempting to define the very concept of language.

So, what is it?

To answer that, we must look at its function. After all, language is a functional tool. The most direct and concise definition is simple: Language is a tool used for communication.

This definition implies two things:

  1. Anything that serves as a tool for communication is a language.
  2. Anything that can be used to communicate is, by definition, a language.

For instance, Chinese is a tool used for communication among Chinese people and those within Chinese cultural spheres. Mathematics is a tool used between humans to communicate our understanding of nature. English serves those within English-speaking nations, as well as outsiders seeking information and culture. Even Python, in the realm of software, is a language used for communication between a human programmer and a computer compiler.

All of these—Chinese, math, English, Python—are languages, yet none of them is the language in its entirety. They are merely components of a vast ecosystem.

So, what is language itself?

A broader, more direct definition emerges: The entirety of all tools used for communication is what we call language. Chinese, English, and mathematics are all just parts; the collective whole is language.

When you ponder this, you realize that different languages intersect. The word tanks from English has now been absorbed into Chinese. Words like typhoon (English), Taifun (German), and typhon (French/Latin) all share the same roots—they are intersections of linguistic evolution. The most immediate and universal intersections are words like “mama” and “papa.”

Similarly, terms like “parallel” or “absolute value” are intersections between Chinese and mathematics. Or rather, mathematics “borrowed” these concepts from natural language, expanding their meanings or inventing new characters, such as 砼 (concrete) in the construction industry. By extension, the specialized jargon of the construction industry is, in its own right, a language.

Ultimately, language is a tool for communication. The subject of that communication is usually human, but not always. We see languages between humans and dogs, humans and machines, and even machines and machines.

How, then, do we categorize language? We can break it down layer by layer.

1. Natural Languages vs. Artificial Languages

  • Natural languages are those that evolved organically through daily human life across different regions. Given that humanity has existed for millions of years with thousands of years of recorded history, natural languages are immensely rich. They cover daily survival—clothing, food, housing, travel—as well as art, emotion, architecture, animals, mathematics, and early sciences.
  • Artificial languages are developed from natural language foundations specifically for a designated field to eliminate ambiguity and boost efficiency.

Take the concept of the horse as an example. In ancient times, horses were vital tools, prompting the written language to develop a massive vocabulary specific to them:

  • Breeds, ages, and genders (驹, 骒, 骘, 骥, 骏, 驽, etc.)
  • Coat colors (骊, 骓, 骝, 骅, 骐, etc.)
  • Movements and states (驰, 骋, 骤, 腾, 驱, etc.)
  • Carts, gear, and systems (驾, 驭, 驷, 骖, 驸, etc.)

While these characters technically belong to the Chinese writing system, they have little to do with the average person. Only specialists needed them. Studying and memorizing all of them is unnecessary and exhausting—much like the classic literary joke about the four different ways to write the character for “return” (回).

Therefore, these characters don’t truly belong to general conversational Chinese; they belong to “Horse Language”—a specialized tool for communicating horse-related information.

If you love Chinese culture but don’t recognize those rare characters, don’t feel guilty. They are merely “Horse Language” parasitic within Chinese. In the exact same way, native English speakers shouldn’t feel guilty for not knowing Python code. Python is just a specialized dialect parasitic within the English-speaking digital ecosystem.

If we map out the landscape of language:

  1. Viewed from a distance, it looks like a continuous, sprawling wetland.
  2. Zooming in, we see distinct regions formed by various natural languages, though they are intricately connected through trade and culture.
  3. Zooming in further, every natural language region has its own rugged terrain and unique micro-ecosystems.
  4. Looking up, we see “mirages”—brilliant conceptual frameworks like Python that form through the optical refraction of multiple cultural traits.

For ordinary people, we only ever use a tiny fraction of natural language, combined with one or two professional domains. In my daily life, I use a slice of Chinese, a slice of English, some programming languages (like Shell and Python), and a bit of management terminology.

2. Why and How We Choose a Language

Our language selection is driven by two factors: ourselves, and the nature of the language.

First, our mother tongue covers our infancy, childhood, and foundational education. It is the tool we naturally rely on to express our inner selves.

Second, as we grow, we inevitably encounter fields that spark our interest, prompting us to master the “language” of that domain—whether it’s animal behavior, Go (the board game), mathematics, or card games. Entering a new linguistic domain always feels like a fresh start. We must learn its unique rules.

Fortunately, artificial languages (especially specialized domain languages) are usually streamlined and inherit the primary traits of their parent natural language. You can grasp the basics of an artificial language in days, hours, or even minutes.

Of course, true mastery takes time, because the ultimate purpose of language is communication. To communicate, you don’t just need to “understand” the language; you must master the rules of using it to connect with others.

Why must we learn mathematics? Because standard everyday vocabulary is insufficient to help us comprehend the universe and articulate our worldview to others. That is the core driving force behind learning any language. (Learning a language purely to show off—like mastering obscure archaic characters—is just intellectual vanity.)

3. Rethinking English

Now, let’s look at English—the language we have studied for over a decade yet often struggle to master. What actually is it?

  1. A natural language that evolved among the people of the British Isles.
  2. Spreads globally through colonization, replacing native tongues in many regions.
  3. Constantly borrowing words from Latin, French, and German to absorb outside cultures.
  4. Expanding exponentially during the Renaissance to accommodate rapid advancements in mechanics, medicine, aesthetics, and architecture.

This reveals that when we “learn English,” we actually have two entirely different objectives:

  1. Learning conversational English: Mastering the natural language to chat with native speakers.
  2. Learning a domain-specific framework carried by English: Such as management, software engineering, or operating systems.

Naturally, these two aren’t strictly mutually exclusive, but we often confuse them.

For example, as I read Les Misérables, I encounter unfamiliar words daily. Most of these aren’t everyday conversational words; they are domain-specific terminology. To truly understand the book, I have to learn them.

Why do I want to read Les Misérables in the first place? Perhaps to understand Victor Hugo, or to comprehend what the French people of 1815 experienced, and how those historical realities apply to my own life.

Could I just read a Chinese translation? I used to think so, but now I know I can’t. Reading the Chinese translation (translated by Li Dan) alongside the English version (translated by Christine) reveals that they feel like completely different books—which implies the French original is likely a third distinct experience.

To genuinely understand the inner world and struggles of a person in 1815 France, relying purely on standard “natural language” is useless. I must understand their psychological terms, the vocabulary of horse-drawn carriages, and the legal language of that era. Without the legal context, it’s impossible to grasp why a mere “yellow passport” carried such a crushing, life-destroying weight for Jean Valjean.

To understand the book, I have to master the micro-languages embedded within it—law, society, and transit.

This is my current framework for language. And this exact understanding is the theoretical foundation of how I use and study languages today.

3. “读《悲惨世界》需要懂法律语言、马车语言、心理学语言”——这是“语言考古学”

你说要真正理解1815年的法国人，必须懂他们的法律语言、社会语言、交通语言。这其实是在说：读经典文学，是在做“语言考古”——你要挖出那个时代的人用哪些词、这些词当时什么意思、哪些词现在已经死了或变了。

这个洞察可以独立成篇：《读〈悲惨世界〉，我在做语言考古》。它会比现在这篇更聚焦、更有读者感。

五、最终点评

这是一篇“有骨架、有血肉、但需要修边”的认知笔记。

  • 骨架：语言=交流工具→自然/人为二分→领域语言寄生→英语学习再定义。这条链是成立的，有解释力。
  • 血肉：马的例子、Python的例子、yellow passport的例子，都是真材实料，不是空谈。
  • 需要修的边：定义要加边界（“表达”+“意义”），分类要三分（自然/领域/形式），英语学习要三分（日常/领域/文学），马的列举要压缩，口语要收一收。

最重要的是：这篇文章的真正价值，不在“语言是什么”的答案，而在你展示了一种认知方式——从日常经验出发，给模糊概念画边界、分层次、建模型，然后用这个模型解释自己的困惑。这和你之前“打工者/创业者思维”“运/动框架”“围点打援”是同一种能力：把经验提炼成可复用的认知架构。
