In Père-Lachaise cemetery, near the common grave, far from the elegant part of that city of tombs, far from all those fanciful monuments that in the presence of eternity display all death’s hideous fashions, in a deserted corner beside an old wall, amid the dandelions and moss beneath a great yew tree with bindweed climbing over it, lies a stone. That stone is no more exempt than any other from the leprous effects of time, from mildew, lichen and bird droppings. Water turns it green, air blackens it. It is not near any path, and people do not like to go there because the grass is high and straight away they get wet feet. When there is a little sunshine, lizards come there. There is all around a rustling of wild oats. In spring, warblers sing in the tree.
