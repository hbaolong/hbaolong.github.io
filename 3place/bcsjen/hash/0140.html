单词学习：《悲惨世界》第五卷第五章

文/韩宝龙

2026年10月27日 01时39分48秒

0003 adept

Pronunciation

/əˈdept/ (uh-DEPT)

Etymology & Root

  • French adepte (from Latin adeptus “having obtained”).

English-to-English explanation

Very skilled or expert in something; highly proficient.

Example:

Hugo was not alone among politicians in expecting that Louis-Napoleon, an adept flatterer, intended to listen to their advice, and who felt both personal resentment and political outrage when he followed his own populist strategy.

0004 arrondissement

Pronunciation

/ar.ənˈdɪs.mənt/ (ar-uhn-DISS-muhnt)

Etymology & Root

  • French arrondissement (from arrondir “to round off”).

English-to-English explanation

A division of a large city or town in France, equivalent to a borough or district.

Example:

In seven years the cost of collecting taxes had dropped by seventy-five per cent in the arrondissement of Montreuil-sur-Mer, which led to this arrondissement being frequently cited above all others by Monsieur de Villèle, then minister of finance.

0005 authority

Pronunciation

/əˈθɔːr.ə.t̬i/ (uh-THOR-ə-tee)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin auctoritas “influence”.

English-to-English explanation

The power or right to give orders; an expert source.

Example:

The extent to which Hugo wove reality into his work is remarkable, lending a documentary authority to his fiction, and the endnotes will, I hope, illuminate this aspect of Hugo’s writing.

0006 cite

Pronunciation

/saɪt/ (SYT)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin citare “summon”.

English-to-English explanation

To quote as evidence or example.

Example:

To the inhabitants of one village he would cite the example of the neighbouring one.

0007 convent

Pronunciation

/ˈkɒn.vənt/ (KON-vuhnt)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French convent (from Latin conventus “assembly”).

English-to-English explanation

A building where a religious community lives together; a religious house or monastery.

Example:

And there is at Peteghem, in Flanders, at the very spot where the Merovingian kings had their summer palace, an Urbanist convent, the abbey of Ste-Claire de Beaulieu, which I saved in 1793.

0008 convey

Pronunciation

/kənˈveɪ/ (kən-VAY)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin conviare “accompany on the way”.

English-to-English explanation

To transport or communicate information or feelings.

Example:

This translation attempts to convey both that historical particularity and that contemporary relevance, that ‘Frenchness’ and that universality, while striving to avoid both unconvincing pastiche and modern intrusions.

0009 disastrous

Pronunciation

/dɪˈzæs.trəs/ (di-ZAS-trəs)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin disastrum “ill-starred”.

English-to-English explanation

Causing great damage or suffering.

Example:

He went into exile and rejected an amnesty, remaining outside France, mainly in the Channel Islands, until the Second Empire collapsed, in September 1870, due to a disastrous war against Prussia.

0010 dislocated

Pronunciation

/dɪsˈləʊ.keɪ.tɪd/ (diss-LOH-kay-tid)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin dis- “apart” + locare “to place” (past participle).

English-to-English explanation

Moved out of place; dislocated (as a joint); disrupted or thrown off track.

Example:

Fauchelevent had dislocated his kneecap in falling.

0011 gardener

Pronunciation

/ˈɡɑː.dən.ər/ (GAR-duh-nuhr)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French jardinier “gardener” (from jardin “garden”).

English-to-English explanation

A person who tends gardens or plants; someone who grows plants or manages gardens.

Example:

He was not as hostile to insects as a gardener might have wished.

0012 gauge

Pronunciation

/ɡeɪdʒ/ (GAYJ)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French jauge “measuring rod”.

English-to-English explanation

To measure or judge; a measuring instrument.

Example:

It may be said this is one infallible gauge of public wealth and poverty: the cost of collecting taxes.

0013 indicator

Pronunciation

/ˈɪn.dɪ.keɪ.tər/ (IN-dih-kay-tuhr)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin indicatus “pointed out” (from indicare “to point out”).

English-to-English explanation

A device that shows information; something that indicates a condition.

Example:

Besides the visible signs we have mentioned of this prosperity created at Montreuil-sur-Mer by Père Madeleine, there was another indicator nonetheless significant for not being visible.

0014 infallible

Pronunciation

/ɪnˈfæl.ə.bəl/ (in-FAL-uh-buhl)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French infallible (from Latin infallibilis “unerring”).

English-to-English explanation

Impossible to make a mistake; completely reliable or trustworthy.

Example:

It would seem to be the case that there exists in certain men a real animal instinct – like all instincts pure and true to itself, one that creates antipathies and sympathies, that inevitably separates one type of person from another, that does not waver, is never perplexed, never silenced and never failing, an instinct clear in its obscurity, infallible, compelling, intractable to all counsel of the intellect and all powers of reasoning against it; an instinct that, however destinies may be shaped, secretly warns the dog-man of the presence of the cat-man and the fox-man of the presence of the lion-man.

0015 infirmary

Pronunciation

/ɪnˈfɜːm.ər.i/ (in-FUR-muh-ree)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French infirmerie (from infirm “weak”).

English-to-English explanation

A hospital or sickroom; a place where the sick or injured are treated.

Example:

Père Madeleine had him conveyed to an infirmary that he had established for his workers in the factory building itself, and which was staffed by two Sisters of Charity.

0016 kneecap

Pronunciation

/ˈniː.kæp/ (Knee-kap)

Etymology & Root

  • Old English cneow-cæp “knee-cap” (from cneow “knee” + cæp “cap”).

English-to-English explanation

The small bone at the front of the knee; a protective pad worn on the knee.

Example:

Fauchelevent had dislocated his kneecap in falling.

0017 prosperity

Pronunciation

/prɑːˈsper.ə.t̬i/ (pros-PER-ə-tee)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin prosperitas “success”.

English-to-English explanation

The state of being successful, especially financially.

Example:

He who has not been a persistent opponent in times of prosperity should remain silent when the downfall comes.

0018 reluctant

Pronunciation

/rɪˈlʌk.tənt/ (ri-LUK-tənt)

Etymology & Root

  • Latin reluctari “struggle against”.

English-to-English explanation

Unwilling and hesitant.

Example:

When the population is hard-pressed, when there is a shortage of work, when business is disastrous, the taxpayer, being short of money, is reluctant to pay taxes, delays as much as possible and then exceeds the respite granted, and the state spends a great deal of money on the costs of enforcement and collection.

0019 sash

Pronunciation

/sæʃ/ (sash)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French ceinture “sash” (from Latin cingere “to bind”).

English-to-English explanation

A long band or scarf worn around the waist; a decorative band.

Example:

The first time Javert saw Monsieur Madeleine wearing the sash that gave him full authority over the town, he felt a shudder of the kind a watch-dog might feel on smelling a wolf in his master’s clothes.

0020 shudder

Pronunciation

/ˈʃʌd.ər/ (SHUH-duhr)

Etymology & Root

  • Middle English shuderen “to tremble” (imitative origin).

English-to-English explanation

To shake or tremble violently, often from fear or cold; a sudden involuntary movement.

Example:

He looked the newcomer up and down, and abruptly exclaimed with a kind of shudder, ‘Would you be the man?’

0021 stiff

Pronunciation

/stɪf/ (STIF)

Etymology & Root

  • Old English stīf.

English-to-English explanation

Not easily bent; formal or unfriendly.

Example:

Fauchelevent recovered, but his knee remained stiff.

0022 wreck

Pronunciation

/rek/ (rek)

Etymology & Root

  • Old French wrec “destruction” (from Germanic wrekan “to destroy”).

English-to-English explanation

A ship or vehicle that has been badly damaged or destroyed; to ruin or damage severely.

Example:

He was a wreck, but flourishing.
